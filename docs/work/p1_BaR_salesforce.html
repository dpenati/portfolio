Leadership case study

Building a High-Performing UX Team

A case study in aligning teams, stakeholders, and products.

I inherited a capable UX team working in a fragmented product environment and focused first on building stability, shared product understanding, and trust across Product and Engineering.

Before asking the team to deliver more,
I focused on changing the conditions around the work. That foundation ultimately made a complete Pipelines redesign possible.
Q2 · Stabilize

Build the team

Create safety, shared ownership, product learning, and a working rhythm the team could trust.

Q3 · Build Credibility

Change the relationship around UX

Make the work visible, bring evidence into decisions, and establish UX as an earlier product partner.

Q4 · Mobilize

Spend the trust

Use the capability and credibility we build to align the organization around an end-to-end Pipelines redesign.

01The Problem
The Problem

The product was fragmented, but the deeper challenge was creating the conditions for the team to work as one.

I inherited a capable UX team supporting a complex Build & Release ecosystem that was being brought into the new Engineering Portal, but designers were working largely within individual product areas. Priorities arrived through different channels, UX was often engaged after key decisions had been made, and there was little shared visibility across the work.

From outside the team, UX looked slow. Inside, the team was navigating competing priorities, late involvement, and unclear decision paths—making it difficult to move quickly without sacrificing rigor.

A

Siloed expertise

Product knowledge was concentrated with individuals, creating dependencies and reinforcing product boundaries.

B

Reactive priorities

Last-minute requests regularly competed with committed work, making focus and planning difficult.

C

UX entered late

Design was often brought in after direction and timelines were already established, limiting its ability to influence the experience.

D

Low organizational trust

UX was not consistently seen as a strategic product partner, and delivery friction was often attributed to the design team.

02The Exploration
The Exploration

Creating the conditions for the team to work as one

When I stepped into management, I already knew the team and the product space well. What I needed to understand more deliberately was which conditions were preventing the team from working as one.

A

Broader product understanding

Expand knowledge beyond individual product areas.

B

Operating confidence and predictability

Create a working rhythm the team could rely on.

C

Visibility and alignment

Make priorities, capacity, and tradeoffs visible across the matrix.

D

Renewed purpose and confidence

Help people reconnect their contribution to the larger product and organization.

A

Broader product understanding

Turn individual expertise into shared capability.

Product knowledge was concentrated in individual areas, and expertise had become closely tied to each person’s sense of relevance and security. Asking people to give up ownership would have created more anxiety, not more collaboration.

Instead, I expanded everyone’s surface area. I paired designers, rotated assignments, and built product learning into the flow of work so each person could deepen their expertise while gaining context beyond their original area.

The shift was from
“my expertise protects me” to “our shared expertise makes us stronger.”
B

Operating confidence and predictability

Create a rhythm the team could rely on.

The team needed a stronger sense of what could realistically be committed, completed, and depended on. I began with short planning cycles that broke the work into smaller, achievable commitments—making progress visible and helping the team build confidence through completion.

I adapted the process to the maturity of the team—not the team to the process.
The weekly rhythm
Monday · CommitmentAlign priorities to capacity and agree on ownership.
Tuesday · LearningShare product knowledge, rationale, and emerging patterns.
Wednesday · CheckpointSurface dependencies, unblock, and recalibrate together.
Friday · DemoShare completed work and connect individual contributions to the broader experience.
C

Visibility and alignment

Make priorities and tradeoffs explicit.

Priorities were coming into the team from multiple directions, often competing with work already underway, and there was no shared view of what UX had committed to or why. That made it difficult for both the team and its partners to plan with confidence.

I began meeting with Product and Engineering leads to understand their roadmaps, priorities, and constraints. Ahead of each quarter, we reviewed scoped work together, aligned it to available UX capacity, and agreed on priorities before work entered the team.

Ad-hoc requests did not disappear, but they became visible tradeoffs rather than private reprioritization.

Protecting focus in practice

When requests came directly to individual designers, I moved prioritization into shared forums rather than restricting access. This allowed us to evaluate new work against capacity, user evidence, engineering dependencies, and existing commitments—with the relevant partners in the conversation.

Protecting focus did not mean creating distance. It meant giving Product, Engineering, and Design a clearer way to make tradeoffs together.
D

Renewed purpose and confidence

Make contribution visible and meaningful.

Frequent leadership changes and repeated feedback about the team’s performance had left people uncertain about what success looked like and whether their contribution was valued.

Friday demos became part of the reset. Everyone on the team presented completed work to peers and invited stakeholders, explaining their thinking, receiving feedback directly, and connecting their contribution to the broader product.

Over time, the demos became a cross-functional alignment point—building confidence inside the team while giving stakeholders a clearer view of the work.

Visibility became a catalyst for trust—both inside and outside the team.
What the exploration revealed

The team changes exposed the product problem.

As designers began working across one another’s areas, the product fragmentation became visible to all. Similar workflows behaved differently, legacy patterns conflicted, and new requests often added another layer of functionality without addressing the underlying experience.

Because the team now had broader product context, we could compare patterns across workflows and see the inconsistencies as a system rather than as isolated issues. That gave us stronger evidence to question feature-by-feature requests and begin reframing the conversation around the experience as a whole.

By the end of Q2, we were not only working differently—we had a much clearer view of the product problem itself.
What became possible next

As confidence and stability grew, I could make more room for work that had previously been difficult to sustain: competitive analysis, pattern reviews, cross-product exploration, and early concept work.

The structure gave the team enough stability to deliver consistently—and enough space to think beyond the immediate work.
03The Solution
The Solution

A different way for UX to operate.

Once the team had a steadier rhythm, I focused on changing how UX participated in the broader organization. Instead of pushing for a larger seat at the table, we made the work more legible, grounded decisions in evidence, and created earlier opportunities for Product, Engineering, and Design to align.

A

Shared capability

Knowledge and ownership were distributed across the team instead of remaining individual territory.

B

Visible collaboration

Work, rationale, risks, and progress became easier for partners to see, discuss, and engage with.

C

Strategic participation

Research and design entered product conversations earlier, while there was still room to shape direction and make meaningful tradeoffs.

Visibility did what advocacy alone could not.
Leadership shift

From designing the answer to designing the conditions

As the team gained confidence, my role changed too. There were moments when it would have been faster to design something myself, but speed was not the goal.

My role became one of calibration: hold the end-to-end vision, give designers meaningful ownership, coach craft and systems thinking, and step in when a local decision risked breaking the larger experience.

My role
  • Clarify the problem.
  • Create the conditions.
  • Distribute ownership.
  • Hold coherence across the system.
I was still designing—just at more than one level.
Then we spent the trust we had earned

By the third quarter, the conditions had changed.

By then, the team had stronger shared product knowledge, more predictable delivery, and greater credibility with Product and Engineering.

That gave us the latitude to redesign the Pipelines experience as a system rather than continue adding to it piece by piece.

Could this way of working hold up under a complete Pipelines redesign in 12 weeks?
How I stayed close to the work

I joined working sessions, reviewed flows and interaction decisions, helped connect local solutions to the broader product system, and challenged assumptions when needed. The goal was to stay close enough to calibrate quality without taking ownership back from the designers.

04The Impact
The Impact

The 12-week redesign became the proof point

The challenge was to move quickly without losing strategic rigor: redesign Pipelines as a coherent system, keep discovery ahead of implementation, and distribute ownership without recreating the silos we had worked to dismantle.

A

Design for coherence and growth

Use the redesign as an opportunity to unify fragmented legacy experiences, establish a consistent end-to-end model, and create room for new capabilities without adding more complexity.

B

Sequence discovery

Reduce ambiguity before implementation commitments hardened, so research, design, and technical feasibility could evolve together.

C

Distribute ownership

Give designers meaningful areas to lead while I stayed accountable for coherence across the broader system.

How the 12-week work was structured

The redesign was organized around six major modules—Evidence, Pipelines List, Single Pipeline, Policies, Tests, and Reporting—with multiple workflows within each. For example, Evidence included artifact management, Promotion, Authorization, and Exemption flows.

What changed

Team

Shared ownership replaced individual territory. Designers had broader product context, greater confidence working across boundaries, and clearer accountability for commitments.

Organization

UX became more visible and predictable. Product and Engineering could engage earlier, see rationale and tradeoffs more clearly, and plan with greater confidence around the team’s work.

Product & Delivery

Previously fragmented Pipelines workflows were redesigned as a connected experience, while the team delivered against the committed GA plan within the execution window.

The deeper impact

Trust—and greater UX agency

The delivery mattered, but the larger outcome was that the team had become more cohesive, partners had greater confidence in how UX worked, and Design was increasingly able to participate while there was still room to shape direction.

The 12-week delivery was not the transformation. It was proof that the transformation had worked.

What I learned

This experience confirmed how I think about design leadership. Predictability does not come from controlling every decision. It came from creating clarity, shared understanding, visible commitments, and enough trust for people to take meaningful ownership.

My job was no longer to have every answer. It was to create the conditions in which the team could make strong decisions together—and to stay close enough to the work to protect coherence and quality.

I’m still a designer. I just design at more than one level now.
