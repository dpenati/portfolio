Building a High-Performing UX Team

A case study in aligning teams, stakeholders, and products

Role: UX Manager Focus: Team Enablement · Cross-Functional Alignment · Predictable Delivery
On this page
Context Strategic Approach Execution Product Impact Reflection

Highlights

  • Built a cohesive UX team with clear ownership, shared standards, and consistent rituals
  • Introduced a lightweight weekly rhythm that improved prioritization and follow-through
  • Increased early visibility via checkpoints and demos, reducing late-stage surprises
  • Turned demos into a cross-functional alignment mechanism that strengthened stakeholder trust
  • Envisioned the product as an end-to-end system
Context

The Conditions I Inherited

In early 2024, I stepped into my first people-management role at JPMorgan Chase, a space that had experienced frequent leadership turnover.

I inherited a four-person UX team supporting Build & Release within the Engineering Portal. In this space, progress depended less on individual effort and more on shared clarity across functions, and it came with challenges typical of large organizations in transition:

T

Distributed team

A distributed team with knowledge spread across individuals, and limited experience operating as a coordinated unit in a multi-stakeholder environment.

P

Fragmented product space

A complex product area with overlapping ownerships and inconsistent patterns, making the end-to-end experience feel disjointed for users.

X

UX engagement late in the cycle

UX engagement often happened late, after key decisions were already made—placing UX in a reactive posture.

A

Alignment gaps

Inconsistent prioritization across Product and Engineering made sequencing unclear, creating parallel asks and fragmenting UX capacity.

These dynamics weren't unique, but they required intentional design leadership. I focused on designing the conditions for alignment, visibility, and coherence—through lightweight interventions that protected focus and improved delivery.

Strategic Approach

Designing for Impact

Rather than treating symptoms in isolation, I focused on designing the conditions that enabled designers, engineers, and product partners to move forward with shared clarity and momentum.

R

Operating rhythm

I introduced lightweight planning and review rituals to create clarity around priorities, sequencing, and delivery—without heavy process. The goal wasn't speed, but predictability and shared momentum.

V

Visible collaboration

I increased visibility into in-progress work and encouraged shared accountability—shifting UX engagement from reactive support to an active partnership with Product and Engineering.

C

Coherent ownership

I articulated a coherent direction early to help the team align, connecting each contribution to the end-to-end system. As shared accountability grew, ownership expanded without reintroducing fragmentation.

Execution

Turning Strategy into Practice

With a clear strategic direction in place, I focused on translating intent into a consistent, observable practice. Rather than introducing change all at once, I established a predictable weekly cadence that balanced focus, learning, and visibility—allowing the team to build new habits incrementally while continuing to deliver.

Weekly Operating Rhythm

I designed a lightweight weekly rhythm around three core activities: planning, doing, and reviewing. This structure created predictable touchpoints for alignment without introducing process overhead.

Mon
Weekly Planning
Quick alignment on priorities, dependencies, and team capacity
Tue–Thu
Focused Execution
Protected time for design work with async check-ins and peer feedback
Fri
Demo & Reflection
Share work-in-progress, gather feedback, celebrate progress
Product Impact

Outcomes

Within six months, the interventions produced measurable shifts in how the team operated and how stakeholders engaged with UX.

3→1
Reduced UX review cycles

Earlier involvement meant fewer late-stage revisions

80%
Demo attendance

Cross-functional partners consistently engaged

5→12
Design system contributions

Team expanded shared ownership of patterns

More importantly, the team developed shared muscle memory for operating as a unit—making decisions collaboratively, surfacing blockers early, and maintaining momentum even as priorities shifted.

Reflection

What I Learned

This experience reinforced that design leadership isn't just about making better products—it's about creating the conditions that enable teams to do their best work. The most impactful interventions weren't grand gestures; they were consistent, lightweight practices that created predictable rhythms and shared accountability.

What stood out was how quickly things improved once we established a predictable cadence. The team didn't need more process—they needed clarity about priorities, consistent touchpoints for alignment, and space to do focused work.

Moving forward, I'm carrying these lessons into future leadership opportunities: start with small, sustainable interventions; protect focus while increasing visibility; and design systems that work with teams, not against them.
